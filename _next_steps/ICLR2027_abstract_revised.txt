Latent diffusion models operate in a latent space whose dimension $d_{\mathrm{latent}}$ typically exceeds the effective signal dimension of the data. We study how latent width affects memorization. For MLP score networks of fixed hidden width trained on synthetic mixtures with known signal dimension, the fraction of memorized samples after 5M steps falls from $30\%$ at $d_{\mathrm{latent}}=5$ to near zero at $d_{\mathrm{latent}}=40$, and the learned score departs from the population score progressively later as the latent widens. The decline survives a dimension-controlled nearest-neighbor test restricted to the true signal subspace. On CelebA and CIFAR-10 VAE latents, comparisons above the VAE identity-preservation threshold show that the mean time to reach $5\%$ memorization increases by $3.6\times$ and $1.4\times$, respectively, while FID follows a separate quality tradeoff. To investigate a possible mechanism, we evaluate the random-feature spectral theory of Bonnaire et al. (2025) at a two-atom covariance. In the separated regime, the feature spectrum has signal, noise-dimension, sample-specific and residual-floor blocks. Under gradient flow, modes learn in parallel, and their relative absorption times are set by spectral edges. Widening lowers the pre-activation variance and shrinks the nonlinear feature mass; holding that variance fixed largely removes the endpoint growth of the measured spectral separation. This mechanism is supported in frozen features, but its causal role in trained networks remains unestablished. A one-parameter onset clock derived from frozen latent representations provides a diagnostic whose predictive advantage over dimension-only baselines is dataset-dependent.
